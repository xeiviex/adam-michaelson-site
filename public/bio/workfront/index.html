Chapter 03 · 2008–2011

Workfront

Product Team Lead

B2B SaaS · 75 to 350 employees · $10M to $100M in annual revenue

Work-management software for enterprise teams. Founded in Utah as AtTask, it rebranded to Workfront and later became part of Adobe’s business suite.

First product hire, recruited to give a fully engineering-led company a product vision and direction for its next phase of growth.

  • $10M → $50Mrevenue in three years
  • 4x · 5xpersona adoption validated
  • 30clients and prospects studied on site
02 · The charter

Give the product a point of view

The charter: vision and strategy for the next phase, in a company that had never had a product manager.

AtTask had always been led by engineering. They hired me as their first product manager to set a vision and strategy for what came next. Steve Ballard joined as head of design and became a mentor in user research.

We pitched the C-team on a strategy built around renewals: deepen adoption among the key personas, grounded in jobs-to-be-done and in immediate value that makes the product sticky. We got buy-in to go find out who those people really were.

  • Mark the executive, who needs visibility.
  • Jen the project manager, our primary persona.
  • Chris the frontline worker, who gets assigned tasks.
03 · The approach

Go sit next to them

Three months, about 30 sites, and a lot of notepads.

We spent roughly three months visiting about 30 clients and non-clients in our target market, among them Nike, Merck, ESPN, and Trane. We split into teams and watched five to ten people per site at their own desks, doing normal work for 30 to 60 minutes each, to see what a phone call with a primary contact never could.

We tested three persona hypotheses. The finding that mattered: frontline workers kept running their work somewhere else, on a notepad, a small whiteboard, or a private spreadsheet. The project manager’s tool was the project manager’s, and they tracked their own work their own way.

04 · Output & outcomes

My Work: a workspace for Chris

A new area of the product, a new data model, and a six-month beta.

We built “My Work,” a workspace for Chris — the frontline worker — plus a team space where he had visibility into the team’s work, could collaborate, and volunteer for work. Work could now flow from Jen to teams (or directly to Chris via a “work request”) and then automatically bubble up to Mark’s dashboards. Research took three months, building and iterating was a few more months, and then a six-month beta with testers drawn from the people we had studied.

One example of the iterative learnings: After launch, we noticed people weren’t moving incoming requests into their workspace. We suspected “Accept” felt like too big a commitment. Changing it to “Work on it” helped people feel ownership rather than obligation, and adoption jumped.

  • 4x adoption by frontline workers (Chris).
  • 5x adoption by project managers (Jen), driven by Chris.
  • 2–3x adoption by executives (Mark).

The results helped drive growth and funding. Read TechCrunch on AtTask’s $13M raise (2012).

05 · Learnings & what’s next

Redesign the whole product, then pick a lane

How the strategy scaled, and why I was ready to leave.

After the success of “My Work,” we redesigned every other area of the product around the same product principles, visual language and flows, and we added social elements like connecting, sharing, and conversation. It meant a new data model and, in effect, a rewrite over the next year or so.

Revenue grew from about $10M to $50M in three years. As we dialed in our ICP work, we noticed the success of quite a few marketing teams in our client-base, and this was what eventually caught the eye of Adobe. With the redesign in place and the growth realized, I was ready for what was next, and Lendio’s founders reached out to me.
