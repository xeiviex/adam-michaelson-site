People

What I hire for, and a hiring pipeline that never stops.

Skills can be taught. These five attributes mostly can't. They're part of a person's character, personality, even their DNA, so they're what I look for first. They're the same for product managers, designers, and engineers.

AffablePeople you want to be around. They're kind, quick to smile, and slow to judge. They're engaging and generally happy, with a light about them. People gravitate to them, and you have a good feeling about them as a human.
SharpAnother kind of light: intelligence. They observe quickly, they're inquisitive, and they grasp new information and principles fast and put them to work.
Empathy and emotional intelligenceThey actively imagine how others feel, put themselves in someone else's place, and work to understand. They know their own faults and are proactive about improving. They're open, not closed.
Proven skillsA portfolio or history of clear success in their craft, which we also verify with a project we give them and assess.
Driven to excellence and outcomesThey're obsessed with getting better at what they do and with the value they create. They sweat the details and the delivered, adopted, verifiable value of their work. They love feedback and confirmation of outcomes.

A world class team

10xThere is a term I use for people who are orders of magnitude more valuable than other employees: those who generate significant value at a fast pace and who have all of the attributes of someone you want to work with. I call them 10xers. They do their best work when talent is given room to work, with protected focus and few interruptions. Read why 10x companies let the talent do their thing.
Hiring is never perfect, so we never stop interviewing.We should always be striving for 10xers. People can arrive as one, or grow into one — as long as they can demonstrate growing into it by an acceptable timeline. As AI takes over more of the work, the value of a person who isn't operating at that level keeps dropping. So I plan for it by setting expectations, giving people time to level up, managing out as needed, and keeping a live hiring pipeline. This is a concept I have adapted from some of the principles outlined by Tony Hsieh in the book Delivering Happiness. Read Tony Hsieh on the Pipeline concept.
New hire check-ins at 30, 60, and 90 daysYou don't fully know what you're getting until you see them work. Every new hire has 30, 60, and 90-day check-ins, and they need to show they have the competencies described above. If they aren't, we rotate them out and bring another candidate in.
Assessing the existing team, leveling up, or replacing as neededAnyone not operating at the 10x level can level up. I give them 30, 60, or 90 days to do it, depending on the situation. If they can't, we replace them. Also, people can decline in output and outcomes over time. When they do, they may need a performance improvement plan.

Principles

Vision

Every great product starts with empathy and understanding of a specific type of person — their environment, their mental models, their problems, and their goals. With that context, we are able to envision an ideal state in the future that represents a time and place where that person's problems are solved and their goals are realized. This creates the “why”↗ behind the product, and the north star that inspires and guides everything we do. To learn more and find out how I create product vision, see here.

Strategy

Once we have a clear vision in terms of ideal state and scope, we turn to the identification and prioritization of the dynamics that will be in opposition to or block our progress toward that vision. As we understand those obstacles, we can choose and design policies that will mitigate risk and the kinds of things we will do to ensure our path toward the vision. This problem identification, policy selection, and action to employ the policy is our product strategy. That strategy may take shape as things we do in our product principles, in our processes, in our GTM, our roadmap, etc. To learn more about product strategy, see here.

Discipline

No matter how experienced, talented, or smart the people are; no matter how interesting the opportunity or product; no matter how strong the position of the company in the market; any team, any product, any company can be displaced and/or will stagnate without deliberate, consistent, and disciplined execution. Discipline in product includes dedication to principles of the craft, to cadences of delivery, and rigorous prioritization in order to drive meaningful and timely ROI. To learn more about discipline in product execution, see here.

Rules of Engagement

The best teams I've worked with have been those where each team member sees themselves and others in two specific ways: 1) part of the collective responsible for the entirety of the outcome (in terms of scope, quality, adoption, and business value), and 2) responsible for a specific area of work and respecting the expertise and authority of the other roles on the team. There is a shared vision and passion for creating something amazing and valuable, and at the same time there is a respect for the various accountabilities across the team. Everyone feels comfortable challenging and contributing broadly, but yields to the authority of the respective expert in each domain. This creates a healthy sense and balance of ownership and respect. Because I feel so strongly about this, I have developed and consistently utilized variations of this framework.

Scope

What we actually build is driven by our vision and the choices we make in the strategy. The vision will guide us to the problems to solve as well as the future we can see and how our company / product fits into / creates that future. This is what I call our “vision scope”, which is usually pretty broad — but has definite boundaries. As we get started, our goal will be to create the minimal version of that scope that also delivers the target value — so that we can validate our solution in terms of delivery on that vision (even if in a small way). This is the true version of MVP: minimum VALUABLE product — not just viable. For more detail on how I approach scope, see here.
