Background and motivation

What draws you to a role, and what are you looking for next?

I love starting new things, and I love taking something good and making it great, in teams as much as in products. Workfront, Aktify and Lendio are examples of each. I'm happiest when there's a real problem worth being passionate about and a team that wants clarity and momentum.

Do you see yourself as better suited to an established business, a startup, or a transformation?

Transformation, based on both my experience and my personality. I love bringing clarity to ambiguity and order to chaos.

Have you worked in a domain like ours before? What if your background isn't a direct match?

Usually the mechanics transfer even when the industry doesn't. Lead generation for lending and for insurance is the same marketplace machine with different suppliers: networks, matching, conversion funnels and monetization. Customer education, milestones and guided experiences in debt relief look a lot like problems I solved at Workfront and BambooHR. Lab software looked unfamiliar until I remembered Workfront's clients included pharma, consumer-goods R&D and regulators, where I watched technicians work onsite and made architecture and UX decisions for configurable lab workflows. I'm clear about what I'd have to learn and I don't pretend to have it already.

What scale of organization have you led?

Lendio grew to about 300 employees and $100M in annual revenue while I was there. At BambooHR and Workfront I led product for platforms serving more than a million users each, and at AltaBank I ran consumer and business online banking. I haven't run a very large engineering organization, so I'm candid that my pattern is building a team from zero and scaling it, or reducing it based on performance, financial need or efficiency gains from AI.

Can you be effective as a leader when the whole company is remote?

In-person is my general preference because the energy is better, but I'm clear on the real question, which is how to be maximally effective as a leader, strategist and executor when everything is remote. I'd get creative about it and be deliberate about building relationships.

Product strategy and vision

How do you create a product vision?

Four workstreams in parallel. First, stakeholder interviews from the board down to the front line. Second, time on-site with customers and prospects. Third, market analysis of competitors and adjacent markets doing interesting things. Fourth, the product itself, from architecture to customer feedback and win/loss. Then I find the patterns, connect the dots, paint the picture and do internal marketing to get everyone onto a North Star. The North Star should be emotionally resonant, a problem worth being passionate about, and quantifiable, so you can tell whether you're moving toward it.

How do you think about strategy?

The most basic, time-honored strategies are usually the ones that work. I like Good Strategy, Bad Strategy for that reason, and I tailor strategy to the specific company and market rather than keeping it generic.

How do you think about moats in an AI-disrupted market? Is data a moat?

I use a simple framework: vision, quality, adoption and economics. Find a real human problem, solve it better than the alternative, drive adoption, and find the unit economics and growth pattern. AI is an enabler and an accelerant, not the solution. Data is a moat if it's something people genuinely need and it's hard to get to, and I fold it into the same framework rather than treating it as a separate source of advantage.

How do you steward a vision when a company has lots of ideas but no strategy?

I take the pile of competing ideas and turn them into strategies aligned to a vision built on what the company already is, its DNA and its core business, rather than far-flung ideas that lose sight of that core. Research first, then bold moves.

How have you transformed a product?

The Workfront story is my go-to. It was built around research: discovering problems and opportunities for innovation, then changing the product's direction based on what we learned.

How do you handle one-off customer requests and highly custom implementations?

I steer away from one-off builds and build configurable systems from the same legos. I prioritize customer "specials" heavily while a product is new and much less once there's market validation and penetration. A one-off is fine when it doesn't hurt usability, perception or real business value, and I still try to build it so it could be reused.

Product managers are strong at research and solution development but bottlenecked releasing to customers. What would you do?

Look at the go-to-market side of release: customer success, partner and lender relations, and especially product marketing. Product marketing is usually the missing muscle. I've run that machine before, so it's an operational answer, not a theoretical one.

How would you build a lending marketplace from scratch?

Three layers: agentic application and intake, integrated third-party data with identity and business verification, and decisioning with automated offers. I'd staff it with lean pods, one forward-deployed product manager per use case paired with one or two AI-assisted engineers who own technical design, code review and QA.

Research and users

How did you know what users wanted when you built a lending marketplace?

I visited small business owners, built a persona for each, affinity-grouped what we learned, and arrived at three needs: options, speed and trust. That research drove every major decision.

What is your design process and approach to user-centered design?

Field research first (about 30 lender site visits and roughly 100 small business owner interviews at Lendio), then validate and calibrate with the same pool plus fresh usability participants, then build, ship, monitor and iterate, roughly a six-month arc to beta. Method first, outcome second.

AI

How do you approach AI as a product leader?

As a way of operating that has been proven over 25 years, with AI wrapped around it for the last six or seven to make it more efficient. Process comes first and AI is the amplifier. AI should create genuine customer value, never be added for its own sake, so I start with vision and problem framing, quality measures and the ideal user experience before choosing technology. And it has to be measured in velocity and ROI.

How do you get more output with fewer people using AI?

By driving AI through the whole workflow. I came back to a team larger than it needed to be and produced more with fewer engineers, fewer PMs and less support. Each person gets AI-assisted tooling, review and QA agents, and shared skills that encode our stack and philosophy.

If PMs can ship with AI coding tools, does collaboration with engineers stop mattering?

No. The PMs who can work that way understand data models, architecture and how to decompose a solution into components. Engineers who cross the other way understand personas, usability and that user experience drives architecture rather than the reverse. I never want to stop collaborating. More heads beat one.

How would you lead on AI as a CPO?

Use it myself, set up a cross-functional group to coordinate internal adoption, use AI in product development, put it into customer-facing products, and share product-and-technology thought leadership. AI governance is a shared responsibility between product and technology.

Leading and building teams

How do you manage and lead large teams?

I optimize for performance and right-size the team for efficiency. I keep a continuous hiring pipeline so the right people are available when a need opens, because if you stop hiring you're assuming you already have all 10x people, which you never do. And I measure people and teams on outputs and outcomes, not activity.

How do you measure product managers and product leaders?

On outputs and outcomes. I set specific daily, weekly and monthly expectations by role, with KPIs that roll up to the North Star and accountability for what the work costs and what revenue it drives. Then a 30/60/90 plan: assess, correct, and either graduate the person or help them move on.

How do you handle a team that needs hard decisions about people?

I lay down clear expectations, measure against them, and help people who aren't performing move on. Front-line people receive this on a spectrum, from "command-and-control" at one end to gratitude at the other. For many it's the first time anyone defined success for them, and they get unlocked, hit a career inflection and come back later thankful. I also think the fear that someone's knowledge walks out the door is usually a fallacy: departures reveal what actually mattered.

Can you change a team that doesn't want to change and still be likeable?

I can do all of it, and I do my best to be likeable. It's hard when you're the one making the difficult changes, especially from the perspective of the person being managed.

How do you work with your team day to day?

Shoulder to shoulder, in the trenches, driven by metrics. I work alongside the team rather than above it.

How do you work with implementation and support teams?

Regular contact, sharing the path forward, and involving them in research and design.

What does great product leadership look like?

Principled decisions calibrated by data. Strategic focus amid disruption and a flood of ideas. Saying no selectively. Attracting excellent product talent. And developing the people you already have.

How do you handle leaders who change direction often?

I'd ask to be hired because I'll push back when necessary: corral scattered initiatives, reduce the number of shiny objects the organization chases, and anchor priorities in a coherent vision backed by research and data. I'm also flexible. Founders and product leadership can agree on explicit thresholds for exceptions so justified pivots are possible without destroying strategic discipline.

Candid questions

What is your greatest weakness?

I come across as bold and absolute, which sometimes stops people from disagreeing with me, and I don't like that. In reality I hold strong opinions loosely and invite debate. Many of my own convictions were formed when other people persuaded me. I actively work to surface dissent because I'm not the smartest person in the room.

Tell me about a time something went wrong.

A fraud incident at Lendio that I turned into a product the company now sells. Separately, an acquired team built a machine-learning underwriting solution that fit our ideal customer poorly. We pivoted to deterministic underwriting rules, and I made the difficult decision to wind that effort down. The lesson was stepping in as a front-line leader and going from zero to one.

How do you say no?

Selectively, and with a reason. I once refused a scope reduction that would have cost quality. With customers, I say no to one-off requests once a product has market validation.
